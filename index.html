The research in plain English

A guide for non-specialists to the March 2026 report “A Family of LLMs Liberated from Static Vocabularies”.

What a tokenizer does

Before a language model reads text, a tool called a tokenizer cuts it into pieces called tokens, like a fixed set of rubber stamps made before training. Common English words have their own stamp. In Aleph Alpha’s example, the German Bundeskanzler needs four pieces while chancellor needs one. The model sees only each stamp’s ID number, so “car” and “cars” can look unrelated.

What HAT does differently

HAT (Hierarchical Autoregressive Transformer) drops the stamp set. A small encoder reads the raw bytes, the units computers use to store letters, and condenses each word into one summary. The backbone, the large main part of the model, works one word at a time. A small decoder writes the reply byte by byte. Because HAT starts from letters, similar spellings get similar summaries.

What the report shows

The report covers models with up to 70 billion parameters, the adjustable numbers a model learns in training. A 7B model was trained from scratch, and Meta’s Llama 3.1 models (8B and 70B) were converted to HAT. Benchmarks are standard tests for comparing models.

  • Tokenizer-free models were competitive with tokenizer-based ones, even when trained on one third of Llama 3.1’s data.
  • German ARC Challenge (school science questions): 59.1% for the 7B model, 47.4% for Llama 3.1 8B.
  • On German text, each backbone step covered 6.03 bytes on average, against 3.33 for Llama 3.1 8B. Fewer steps can mean less computing.
  • The converted 8B chat model won 71.0% of English and 73.9% of German MT-Bench comparisons, scored by an AI judge, against Llama 3.1 8B Instruct.

Why it matters

Tokenizers are tuned mostly for English, so German and specialist terms, as in contracts or technical specifications, break into more pieces. Because HAT reads letters, the report says it copes better with spelling differences such as typos and adapts to new languages and fields with further training.

What it does not show yet

The models cover English and German only and were not tuned for code or maths. Under heavy load, current serving software produces less output per second than with a comparable tokenizer-based model. The 70B model is experimental and scores lower than Llama 3.3 70B Instruct on some academic tests. The authors say the robustness benefits need more testing.

Who can use the models

Six models and 200 checkpoints (snapshots saved during training) are free on Hugging Face under the Open Aleph License. A license sets the legal terms of use, and this one covers only non-commercial, non-administrative uses such as research and teaching.

Sample release kit

Sample drafts I would prepare for the team’s review. They are not official Aleph Alpha communications.

Sample draft · LinkedIn post

Announcing the report

Can a language model work without a tokenizer and still compete? Our new report describes tokenizer-free HAT models with up to 70 billion parameters, trained in English and German.

Two findings:

→ Our 7B model, trained from scratch on one third of Llama 3.1’s data budget, is competitive with Llama 3.1 8B.
→ On German text our models need fewer steps: 6.03 bytes per step on a German knowledge test, compared with 3.33 for Llama 3.1 8B.

Models, 200 training checkpoints and our evaluation framework are public, so you can check the numbers yourself.

Report: arxiv.org/abs/2603.15953
Models: huggingface.co/Aleph-Alpha

Sample draft · Conference talk abstract

Training language models without a tokenizer: lessons from HAT models up to 70B parameters

Most language models depend on a tokenizer, a fixed vocabulary learned before training and usually tuned for English. We present the Hierarchical Autoregressive Transformer (HAT), in which small byte-level encoder and decoder modules surround a word-level backbone. We compare two routes: training a 7B model from scratch on nearly 4 trillion words, and converting pre-trained Llama 3.1 8B and 70B models. We share English and German benchmark results, compression gains and what it took to serve hierarchical models in vLLM. We close with open questions and our 200 released checkpoints for studying how models learn.

Sample draft · 5-slide internal summary

Slide outline

  1. Why: fixed tokenizers split German and specialist words into many pieces.
  2. How: HAT reads bytes, works in words and writes bytes, with no fixed vocabulary.
  3. Results: German ARC Challenge 59.1% vs 47.4%; 6.03 vs 3.33 bytes per step on German text.
  4. Limits: English and German only, no code or maths focus, 70B is experimental.
  5. Release: six models, 200 checkpoints and eval-framework, with license terms.

What other research labs do

  • Cohere Labs runs an Open Science Community with virtual talks and research groups, for example on multilingual AI. Its Scholars Program is a paid, remote-first position that pairs emerging researchers with mentors.
  • EleutherAI, a non-profit lab, works mainly in a public Discord server shared by staff, volunteers and outside collaborators. The March 2026 report compares its 200 checkpoints to the 154 per model in EleutherAI’s Pythia suite.
  • Ai2 releases its Olmo models with training data, code and checkpoints, and its Olmo page shows projects others built with them.

Ideas I would explore

These are ideas to test with the team, not plans or promises.

  1. A checkpoint study circle. The report says the team has not yet analysed the 200 checkpoints. Inviting a few university groups to study them, with findings collected on one public page, would test whether outside researchers pick them up.
  2. A German evaluation sprint. eval-framework has over 90 tasks and MTBench-German is public. A one-day online sprint for student teams could show whether shared tools attract new contributors.
  3. An open spelling test. eval-framework supports perturbation testing, which changes inputs on purpose, for example with typos. A public notebook comparing HAT and tokenizer-based models on misspelled German text would let anyone repeat the test.
  4. “Built with GermanWeb”. Aleph-Alpha-GermanWeb had 898 downloads in 30 days, all anonymous. An optional form on the dataset card asking what people built could turn some users into case studies.
  5. Plain-language summaries. Technical blog posts could get a one-page summary in English and German, like the explainer above. Comparing reading time and shares with and without one would show whether it helps.
